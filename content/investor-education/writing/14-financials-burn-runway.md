# Financials, burn, and runway

Financial statements are a snapshot and history, not a promise of future performance. Revenue shows sales recognized under the company’s accounting method; cash flow tracks money moving in and out. A company can report revenue while still losing money or have cash that is already committed to bills. Check statement dates, accounting notes, debt, unpaid obligations, and whether one customer supplies a large share of sales.

A fictional calculation: the company begins with $180,000 in cash, spends $60,000 monthly on operations, and receives $30,000 in monthly cash receipts. Net burn is $60,000 minus $30,000, or $30,000 per month. Dividing $180,000 by $30,000 gives six months of simple runway. This assumes cash in and out remain steady, which they rarely do exactly. It excludes one-time costs, delayed customer payments, debt service, changes in spending, and any new financing.

Ask what the company expects to do with the offering proceeds and what milestones the money is meant to fund. A funding round may extend runway, but it does not prove the business will reach profitability or secure another round. If the company’s stated runway differs from a simple cash-and-burn calculation, ask what assumptions explain the difference. Avoid treating an approximate runway as a countdown to guaranteed success; it is one metric to understand alongside sales, margins, retention, obligations, and execution risks.
