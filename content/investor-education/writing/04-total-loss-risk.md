# You can lose the entire investment

A risk warning is not just formal language. It describes conditions that can change whether the company survives and whether a security has value. Common risks include delayed product launches, weak demand, cash shortages, founder departures, unexpected costs, new competitors, and legal or regulatory problems. A risk factor does not prove a company will fail, but ignoring it removes useful context.

Consider this fictional example. Lena invests $500 in a young food company. The company later closes after spending its cash on inventory and payroll. If the remaining assets are used to pay secured lenders, employees, taxes, and other claims ahead of Lena’s security, there may be no money left for her. Her investment recovery could be $0. That does not mean every startup loses money, and it does not justify inventing a failure-rate statistic. It means a full loss is a realistic possibility to plan for.

Read the offering’s company-specific risks and compare them with the business plan. If success depends on one customer, one license, one supplier, or another financing round, understand that dependency. Check how much capital is being raised and whether the plan changes if the company raises less than its maximum. Diversification can reduce the impact of one company’s failure, but multiple startup investments can still lose value together. Do not use rent, emergency savings, or money needed for near-term obligations.

*Educational information only. This is not legal, tax, or investment advice. Examples are fictional and simplified.*
