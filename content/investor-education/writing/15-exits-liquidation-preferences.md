# Exits and liquidation preferences

An acquisition or other exit can provide a possible source of proceeds, but investors do not necessarily receive cash according to a simple ownership percentage. The company’s governing documents define how proceeds are distributed. Secured debt, transaction expenses, and preferred-stock rights can affect what remains. A liquidation preference gives a preferred class a stated priority in certain liquidation events, including some sale transactions.

Fictional simplified example: preferred investors contributed $2 million and hold a one-times non-participating liquidation preference. If the company is sold for $1.5 million and there are no higher-priority claims or expenses, they could receive the available $1.5 million, leaving $0 for common shareholders. At a $12 million sale, suppose those preferred investors would own 20% if they converted to common. Twenty percent of $12 million is $2.4 million, which is more than their $2 million preference, so conversion might be economically preferable under these assumptions.

This example leaves out debt, fees, multiple preferred rounds, participating preferences, caps, and contractual details. The exact waterfall may differ substantially. A SAFE investor may also have specific sale or dissolution provisions rather than ordinary shareholder rights. Read the security and company capitalization materials to understand which classes rank ahead of yours and what conversion choices exist. Do not infer an investor payout from the company’s headline valuation alone. Ask a qualified professional to explain complex preference language.

*Educational information only. This is not legal, tax, or investment advice. Examples are fictional and simplified.*
