# Dilution when a company issues more shares

A company’s ownership is usually expressed as a fraction of its total shares or a fully diluted share count. When new shares are issued, an existing holder’s fraction can shrink unless that holder also buys enough shares to maintain the same percentage. This is dilution. It is a change in percentage ownership, not an automatic calculation of investment loss; the company’s value and each security’s rights also matter.

Fictional example: Noor owns 100 shares of a company with 1,000 shares outstanding, so she owns 10%. The company issues 500 new shares to finance product development. Noor still has 100 shares, but the total is now 1,500. Her stake is 100 divided by 1,500, or about 6.67%. If the financing helps the business grow, her smaller percentage could still represent a more valuable stake. If the money is spent without progress, that may not happen.

Look beyond issued common shares. SAFEs and convertible notes may turn into shares in a future round. Employee option pools may also affect a fully diluted calculation. Preferred stock can have liquidation or voting rights different from common stock. Ask whether a company’s percentages are calculated before or after conversion and whether the option pool is included. One headline percentage, without the assumptions behind it, can be misleading. Future financing terms can change ownership again.
