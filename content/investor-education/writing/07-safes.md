# What a SAFE does and does not mean

A SAFE is a contractual right to receive future equity if the agreement’s conditions are met. The SEC’s investor bulletin emphasizes that a SAFE is not common stock at the time of investment and may never convert. The company could avoid the particular triggering event specified in the SAFE. Different forms can handle financings, acquisitions, and dissolution differently.

For a fictional investor putting $500 into a SAFE, the investor does not automatically own a known percentage of the company on signing day. A valuation cap is one input to a later conversion-price calculation. The eventual share count may depend on the next financing, the company’s capitalization definition, other SAFEs, options, and the precise contract. A cap is not a promise that the company will be valued at that amount or that the investor will own a particular fraction.

Read the exact instrument, not just a slide or platform summary. Identify what financing triggers conversion, what share class is issued, how a sale or shutdown is treated, and whether there are other outstanding securities that could affect conversion. The SEC warns that a SAFE may not be “simple” or “safe.” Some SAFEs are not debt and do not promise repayment. If you cannot explain the trigger and possible outcomes in your own words, pause and consult a lawyer or other qualified professional. This is education, not advice on a particular SAFE.
