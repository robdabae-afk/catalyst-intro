# Startup investing basics

Startup investing is different from buying shares of a public company. A startup is privately held, so there may be no exchange or ready buyer for its securities. You also may receive fewer ongoing updates than a public-company shareholder. The exact rights depend on the security and the legal documents, not on a marketing description.

A fictional example: Maya invests $500 in a young software company through an offering. If the company later succeeds, her security might gain value, but she does not automatically get $500 back plus a profit. If the company shuts down with no money left after debts, her investment could be worth zero. If the company grows but never has a sale or public listing, she may still have no practical way to sell.

Before considering an offering, ask what security is being sold, how the price was set, what happens if the company raises more money, and what rights apply if it is sold or closes. Read the company’s disclosures and risk factors, including how it plans to use proceeds. Be cautious about putting in money needed for rent, debt payments, emergencies, or near-term goals. A startup investment can be speculative and illiquid even when the business looks promising. No historical growth story guarantees a future result.

*Educational information only. This is not legal, tax, or investment advice. Examples are fictional and simplified.*
