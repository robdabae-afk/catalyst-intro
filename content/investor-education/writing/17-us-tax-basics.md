# U.S. tax basics for startup securities

This lesson covers broad U.S. federal tax concepts, not a tax result for any particular investor. The IRS generally treats investment stocks and bonds as capital assets, and the amount realized from a sale is compared with adjusted basis to determine gain or loss. Holding period matters: assets held for more than one year before disposition are generally long-term, while assets held one year or less are generally short-term. Tax rates and reporting can depend on income, the security, and other facts.

Fictional arithmetic example: Ari buys stock for $500 and later sells it for $800. Before fees or basis adjustments, the difference is $300. That does not tell Ari the final tax owed. A sale could be reported differently based on holding period and other transactions. If the investment is a SAFE, later converts, receives distributions, is acquired, or becomes worthless, the tax analysis may be more complex. Merely seeing a company’s value decline does not necessarily create a deductible loss. Worthless securities have specific IRS rules and reporting requirements.

Keep the investment agreement, proof of payment, conversion records, statements, sale records, and any tax forms. Do not assume a startup investment qualifies for specialized tax treatment because of the company’s age or security label; eligibility depends on the facts and tax rules. State tax treatment can differ from federal treatment. For an actual transaction, consult a qualified tax professional who can review the documents and your filing situation.

*Educational information only. This is not legal, tax, or investment advice. Examples are fictional and simplified.*
