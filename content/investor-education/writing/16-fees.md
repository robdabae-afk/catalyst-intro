# Fees and how they change the math

Fees affect the economics of an investment, and the label “fee” may cover more than one charge. The SEC advises investors to review fee disclosures and statements because even small costs can matter over time. Startup offerings can have different arrangements: the company may pay the platform or payment costs, the investor may pay a disclosed charge, or both may apply. The terms are offering-specific.

Here is a fictional illustration using a 3% investor charge plus a $1 fixed fee. If the investor commits $1,000 and the fees are charged on top, 3% of $1,000 is $30, plus $1, so the payment is $1,031. If those same fees are deducted from a $1,000 commitment, $969 would remain for the investment. This is only arithmetic; the fee rate is invented and not a typical or promised fee.

Before committing, check whether the displayed amount is the amount invested or the total payment. Look for ongoing servicing, fund-level, or administration expenses if the offering uses a pooled vehicle. Find out what happens to fees if the offering is cancelled, fails to meet its target, or an investment is refunded. Compare the fee disclosures with the checkout screen and save the confirmation. A fee that seems small can still matter, but paying no fee does not make a risky investment safer.

*Educational information only. This is not legal, tax, or investment advice. Examples are fictional and simplified.*
